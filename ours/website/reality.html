The real world

From one person's nod to a whole team hearing the command

Picture team Alpha and team Charlie on an operation, each member wearing AirPods. Speaking or signalling with a hand would give them away. Alpha 2 sees movement on the left, tilts left and nods down. Every teammate hears "Alpha 2: enemy left" in their ears.

How it would work

The central platform (hub)

Knows who is in which team and online (battery, which earbud streams), holds each team's dictionary, routes each command to the right people, shows a live timeline with acknowledgements, and keeps an after-action log. A "not me" button turns every false command into a labelled example for retraining.

The demo we can build now

The Mac runs the recogniser and the hub. Teammates' phones (any phone) join the same Wi-Fi from a phone hotspot (no internet needed), open a web page, hear the command and tap to acknowledge. An iPhone app is optional, later.

In the field, later

The same small message could travel over a mesh radio or LTE, and plug into ATAK, the team map app many forces already use (it has a mesh-radio plugin).

Body turn vs head turn: acceleration is the key

An earbud cannot see the body, so turning at a corner and turning the head look alike in the rotation signal. Two cues separate them:

  1. Net heading. A gesture goes out and comes back: P01's turns went 60-80° out and returned to within about 3°. A body turn at a corner changes the heading and stays. The heading comes from the AirPods' orientation (quaternion), not from adding up rotation rate, which gives a false 55-72° for circles.
  2. Acceleration head rotation cannot explain. The earbud sits beside the neck's rotation axis, so a pure head turn already accelerates it (P01: 4.5-6.4 m/s² peak). Treating the head as rigid, aear = abody + α × r + ω × (ω × r). Fitting r on half of P01's seated trials gave 8.6 cm to the side, which is where the ear is, found from data alone. On the other half, head rotation explained 91-92 % of the acceleration in turns. Whatever is left over is the body moving, so detection can get stricter as that leftover grows, and switch off while running.
One person, 14 trials, seated. Even with a still body the leftover peaked at 1.5-2.2 m/s², so the separation has to be shown on the walking recordings. Script: algorithm/scripts/lever_arm_check.py.

Covertness: what we can actually measure

Nobody outside the team knows the dictionary, so the question is not "which command was it?" but "was a command sent at all?" Two measures:

  • Observers: short video clips, half with a command and half with ordinary head movement; observers answer yes or no. Covert means their accuracy is near chance (50 %), reported as d′.
  • From the data alone: compare each command's angle, speed and duration with everyday head movement in the no-gesture recordings. Commands inside the everyday range are objectively inconspicuous.
Covertness and false commands are two sides of one coin: the more a command looks like normal movement, the more normal movement looks like a command. The plot of accuracy and false commands against gesture size is one of the paper's key figures.
